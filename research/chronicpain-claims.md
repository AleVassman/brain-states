## What is reconstruction in this episode

The evidence comes from separate studies: one that followed people with recent back pain for a year, pooled analyses of how the brain responds to pain, and a study of people with chronic back pain doing a simple task. Chronic pain comes in many forms, and different types leave different traces in the brain, so this is not one pattern for everyone. An international expert group warns that brain scans cannot be used as a test for whether someone has chronic pain. The loss of grey matter in people whose pain persisted is a change in tissue and is described, not drawn.

## Notes from the research pass

- The pain response is drawn identical in both brains. Tanasescu et al. 2016 (full text): "Meta-analysis of contrast maps showed no difference between CP, controls, mood conditions." They did find "focally increased activation likelihood, but unchanged intensity, in CP when stimulated at the most painful body part" (left putamen, left frontal gyrus, right insula); because the intensity was unchanged, that is not drawn as brighter.
- Apkarian et al. 2005 (abstract) report that "chronic pain engages brain regions critical for cognitive/emotional assessments". The abstract does not name them, so they are not drawn.
- The typical variant in the first two phases stands for people whose back pain resolved (Baliki et al. 2012 compared them with people whose pain persisted).
- The resting network is drawn below rest during the task (quietening) in both brains, less so with chronic pain. The size of the quietening is illustrative; Baliki et al. 2008 report "reduced deactivation" without a figure in the abstract.
- The secondary body-sense cortex and the mid-cingulate are named in the sources but are not separate regions in the brain model.
- Davis et al. 2017 (abstract): "the use of brain imaging findings to support or dispute a claim of chronic pain - effectively as a pain lie detector - is not warranted". Reflected in the caveat.
- Read in full: Tanasescu 2016, Baliki 2012. Read as abstracts only: Baliki 2008, 2011, Apkarian 2005, Al-Faraj 2026, Davis 2017.

## The condition

### **insula** — Well established

Claim: Part of the brain's response to a painful touch in everyone. In a pooled analysis of many kinds of chronic pain, the overall pattern of response was the same as in people without it, and 18 direct comparisons found no consistent difference in strength; the authors note subtle differences, or differences in particular groups, cannot be ruled out. When the most painful part of the body was touched, the right insula was more often active in people with chronic pain, though not more strongly. Drawn the same in both.

### **nucleus-accumbens → mpfc** (connection) — Mixed evidence

Claim: In people with recent back pain, a tighter link between the nucleus accumbens and the medial prefrontal cortex predicted that the pain would persist a year later, and the link was still tighter at one year. The authors take this to mean the circuit is involved in pain becoming chronic. It was measured at the start and at one year; it is shown held after that, which was not measured. From one study.
