## What is reconstruction in this episode

Spontaneous panic attacks are almost never caught in a scanner, so this episode is built from responses to carbon dioxide in the scanner, from reviews of scans of people with panic disorder doing other tasks, from a classic model based on fear learning in animals, and from one study of healthy volunteers being chased by a virtual predator. These were never one experiment. The role of the amygdala is unresolved: the classic model puts it at the centre, later reviews find it less consistently than predicted, and three people with no working amygdala had panic attacks when given carbon dioxide, more readily than other people, which suggests the amygdala may normally hold panic back. The brainstem is drawn as a rough outline of the whole structure because the small nuclei thought to be involved are not in the brain model. How an attack subsides has not been studied in scans.

## Notes from the research pass

- No typical-response comparison. The one group comparison read (Goossens et al. 2014, abstract) reports a single difference, a larger brainstem response in panic disorder, and reports the insula finding as the same in all groups. That is not enough to draw a second brain.
- Not drawn, because the sources give no direction for a panic attack: the cingulate cortex (reviews say it behaves "differently") and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (the shift away from it was measured in healthy volunteers facing an outside threat).
- No lines are drawn. The amygdala-to-hypothalamus pathway of the classic model is from animal fear learning, and panic can occur without the amygdala.
- The periaqueductal grey would be the structure to show for the threat-closing-in finding (Mobbs et al. 2007). Its atlas file still has to be downloaded by hand.
- The episode is the carbon-dioxide kind of panic because that is what has been scanned. Whether spontaneous attacks follow the same course is not known.
- Read as abstracts only: Goossens 2014, Sobanski & Wagner 2017, Gorman 2000, Dresler 2013. Read in full: Feinstein 2013, Mobbs 2007.

## Order of events

1. **A signal from the body** (seconds) — start of the episode.
2. **Alarm** (seconds) — order **reconstructed**: The breathing study averaged over two minutes of carbon dioxide and did not time the regions against each other. That the brainstem signal comes before the felt alarm is an inference from the body signal being the trigger.
3. **Peak** (minutes) — order **reconstructed**: The shift towards the midbrain was measured in healthy volunteers as a virtual predator approached, not during panic. Using it for the peak of a panic attack is an analogy.
4. **Subsiding** (minutes (not studied in scans)) — order **reconstructed**: No source read for this episode followed the end of a panic attack. The return to baseline is assumed.

## The condition

### **brainstem** — Mixed evidence

Claim: More active in people with panic disorder than in others when they breathe air with extra carbon dioxide, in one study read as an abstract. A review also lists the brainstem in the network that behaves differently in panic disorder. In healthy volunteers, a midbrain area inside the brainstem, the periaqueductal grey, becomes active as a threat closes in, more so the greater the dread; that is not a panic finding and the area is not drawn separately.

### **insula** — Mixed evidence

Claim: The front of the insula became more active the more uncomfortable breathing felt, in people with and without panic disorder alike: no difference between the groups was reported. Reviews include the insula in the network that behaves differently in panic disorder.

### **amygdala** — Still debated

Claim: Drawn at baseline, because the sources disagree on what it does. The classic model of panic puts the amygdala at the centre, by analogy with fear learning in animals, and says so as speculation. Later reviews find it less consistently than that model predicts. Three people with no working amygdala on either side had panic attacks when given carbon dioxide, and did so more readily than other people, which the authors take to suggest that the amygdala may normally hold panic back.

### **hypothalamus** — Still debated

Claim: Evidence mainly from animals: there, signals reaching the hypothalamus and brainstem produce the bodily signs of fear, such as a racing heart. That the same happens in a human panic attack is the classic model's speculation, and nothing read for this episode measured it in people.
