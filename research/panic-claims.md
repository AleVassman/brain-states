# Panic attack: an alarm from inside the body — claims and sources

Owner sign-off: **not yet reviewed**

Generated from `research/panic.mjs`. Every claim the episode makes is listed with the exact
sentence that supports it. Activity values are qualitative: direction and rough relative size only.

## What is reconstruction in this episode

Spontaneous panic attacks are almost never caught in a scanner, so this episode is built from attacks provoked with carbon dioxide, from scans of people with panic disorder doing other tasks, from a classic model based on fear learning in animals, and from one study of healthy volunteers being chased by a virtual predator. These were never one experiment. The role of the amygdala is disputed: the classic model puts it at the centre, later reviews find it less consistently than predicted, and three people with no working amygdala had panic attacks when given carbon dioxide. The brainstem is drawn as a rough outline of the whole structure because the small nuclei thought to be involved are not in the brain model. How an attack subsides has not been studied in scans.

## Notes from the research pass

- The episode is the carbon-dioxide kind of panic because that is what has been scanned. Whether spontaneous attacks follow the same course is not known.
- The periaqueductal grey would be the right structure to light at the peak (Mobbs et al. 2007). Its atlas file still has to be downloaded by hand, so the whole brainstem outline is lit instead and the text says so.
- The locus coeruleus, part of the classic fear network (Sobanski & Wagner 2017), is too small to draw.
- The direction of the cingulate change during an attack is not stated in the sources read; it is drawn as an increase and labelled as an assumption.
- Read as abstracts only: Goossens 2014, Sobanski & Wagner 2017, Gorman 2000, Dresler 2013. Read in full: Feinstein 2013, Mobbs 2007.

## Order of events

1. **A signal from the body** (seconds) — start of the episode.
2. **Alarm** (seconds) — order **reconstructed**: The breathing study averaged over two minutes of carbon dioxide and did not time the regions against each other. That the brainstem signal comes before the felt alarm is an inference from the body signal being the trigger.
3. **Peak** (minutes) — order **reconstructed**: The shift towards the midbrain was measured in healthy volunteers as a virtual predator approached, not during panic. Using it for the peak of a panic attack is an analogy.
4. **Subsiding** (minutes (not studied in scans)) — order **reconstructed**: No source read for this episode followed the end of a panic attack. The return to baseline is assumed.

## The condition

### **brainstem** — Mixed evidence

Claim: More active in people with panic disorder than in others when they breathe air with extra carbon dioxide, in one study read as an abstract. A review also lists the brainstem in the network that behaves differently in panic disorder. In healthy volunteers, a midbrain area inside the brainstem, the periaqueductal grey, becomes active as a threat closes in and more so the greater the dread; it is not drawn separately.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.9), ends above baseline (0.1).

- Goossens L, Leibold N, Peeters R, Esquivel G, Knuts I, Backes W, Marcelis M, Hofman P, Griez E, Schruers K (2014). Brainstem response to hypercapnia: a symptom provocation study into the pathophysiology of panic disorder. doi:10.1177/0269881114527363 — study, abstract-only
  > "Patients show increased brainstem activation in response to hypercapnia compared to controls and divers."
- Sobanski T, Wagner G (2017). Functional neuroanatomy in panic disorder: Status quo of the research. doi:10.5498/wjp.v7.i1.12 — review, abstract-only
  > "recent functional studies suggest abnormal activation mainly in an extended fear network comprising brainstem, anterior and midcingulate cortex (ACC and MCC), insula, and lateral as well as medial parts of the prefrontal cortex"
- Mobbs D, Petrovic P, Marchant JL, Hassabis D, Weiskopf N, Seymour B, Dolan RJ, Frith CD (2007). When fear is near: threat imminence elicits prefrontal-periaqueductal gray shifts in humans. doi:10.1126/science.1144298 — study, full-text
  > "as the virtual predator grew closer, brain activity shifted from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex to the periaqueductal gray"
- Mobbs D, Petrovic P, Marchant JL, Hassabis D, Weiskopf N, Seymour B, Dolan RJ, Frith CD (2007). When fear is near: threat imminence elicits prefrontal-periaqueductal gray shifts in humans. doi:10.1126/science.1144298 — study, full-text
  > "imminence-driven periaqueductal gray activity correlated with increased subjective degree of dread and decreased confidence of escape"

### **insula** — Mixed evidence

Claim: The front of the insula became more active the more uncomfortable breathing felt, in people with and without panic disorder. Reviews include the insula in the network that behaves differently in panic disorder.

### **amygdala** — Still debated

Claim: The classic model of panic puts the amygdala at the centre, by analogy with fear learning in animals, and says so as speculation. Later reviews find it less consistently than that model predicts, and more during an anxious state than as a lasting trait. Three people with no working amygdala on either side still had panic attacks when given carbon dioxide, so it is not needed for this kind of panic.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.55), ends at baseline (0.05).

- Gorman JM, Kent JM, Sullivan GM, Coplan JD (2000). Neuroanatomical hypothesis of panic disorder, revised. doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.157.4.493 — review, abstract-only
  > "It is speculated that a similar network is involved in panic disorder."
- Sobanski T, Wagner G (2017). Functional neuroanatomy in panic disorder: Status quo of the research. doi:10.5498/wjp.v7.i1.12 — review, abstract-only
  > "differences in the amygdala activation were not as consistently reported as one would predict from the hypothesis of Gorman et al (2000)"
- Dresler T, Guhn A, Tupak SV, Ehlis AC, Herrmann MJ, Fallgatter AJ, Deckert J, Domschke K (2013). Revise the revised? New dimensions of the neuroanatomical hypothesis of panic disorder. doi:10.1007/s00702-012-0811-1 — review, abstract-only
  > "The assumption of a generally hyperactive amygdala in PD seems to apply more to state than trait characteristics of PD, and involvement of further areas in the fear circuit, such as anterior cingulate and insula, is suggested."

### **hypothalamus** — Still debated

Claim: In animals, signals from the amygdala to the hypothalamus and brainstem produce the bodily signs of fear, such as a racing heart. That the same happens in a human panic attack is the classic model's speculation.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.5), ends at baseline (0).

- Gorman JM, Kent JM, Sullivan GM, Coplan JD (2000). Neuroanatomical hypothesis of panic disorder, revised. doi:10.1176/appi.ajp.157.4.493 — review, abstract-only
  > "Projections from the amygdala to hypothalamic and brainstem sites explain many of the observed signs of conditioned fear responses."

### **vmpfc** — Still debated
