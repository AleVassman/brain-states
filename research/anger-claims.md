## What is reconstruction in this episode

Researchers describe the brain basis of anger as still a matter of debate. The deep "threat system" shown early in the episode is known mainly from animal experiments and is assumed, not shown, to work the same way in people; its third part, the periaqueductal grey, is not drawn because its shape could not be obtained for this project. The findings about feeling angry and about dwelling on it come partly from a single study in which volunteers were insulted in a scanner. Dwelling and regulating are shown together at the end, but a person may do one, the other, or both. All four sources except one review were read as abstracts only.

## Notes from the research pass

- No typical-response comparison. Anger is an emotion, not a condition. The nearest comparison in the literature, people with a history of impulsive aggression (Coccaro et al. 2007), rests on ten patients and ten controls looking at angry faces; it is described in the text of the relevant regions instead of being drawn as a separate brain.
- The meta-analysis this episode leans on opens with: "The neural bases of anger are still a matter of debate." (Sorella et al. 2021, abstract).
- The periaqueductal grey is part of the threat system described by Blair (2012) but is not drawn: its atlas file could not be downloaded automatically. If it is added later, it belongs after the hypothalamus, marked "debated".
- Read as abstracts only: Sorella 2021, Denson 2009, Coccaro 2007. Read in full: Blair 2012.

## Order of events

1. **Provocation** (a second or two) — start of the episode.
2. **Threat response** (seconds) — order **reconstructed**: The sequence from amygdala to hypothalamus is traced in animals, where it escalates as a threat gets closer. Placing it before the conscious feeling of anger is an inference from that work.
3. **Feeling angry** (seconds to minutes) — order **reconstructed**: Brain scans of anger cannot separate events closer than a few seconds, so the feeling is placed after the first reaction by inference, not by measurement.
4. **Dwelling or reining in** (minutes) — order **reconstructed**: In the study this rests on, volunteers were insulted first and prompted to dwell on it afterwards. The order follows that design.

## The condition

### **amygdala** — Mixed evidence

Claim: Responds when people perceive anger in others, such as an angry face, and is treated as the first stage of the threat system behind reactive aggression. A pooled analysis of studies of feeling angry, as opposed to seeing anger, did not find the amygdala. In one small study of people with a history of impulsive aggression, the amygdala reacted more strongly to angry faces.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.65), ends above baseline (0.2).

- Sorella S, Grecucci A, Piretti L, Job R (2021). Do anger perception and the experience of anger share common neural mechanisms? Coordinate-based meta-analytic evidence of similar and different mechanisms from functional neuroimaging studies. doi:10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.117777 — meta-analysis, abstract-only
  > "Anger perception analysis resulted in significant activation in the amygdala"
- Blair RJR (2012). Considering anger from a cognitive neuroscience perspective. doi:10.1002/wcs.154 — review, full-text
  > "neural systems implicated in reactive aggression (amygdala, hypothalamus and periaqueductal gray; the basic threat system) are critically implicated in anger"
- Coccaro EF, McCloskey MS, Fitzgerald DA, Phan KL (2007). Amygdala and orbitofrontal reactivity to social threat in individuals with impulsive aggression. doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2006.08.024 — study, abstract-only
  > "Relative to controls, individuals with IED exhibited exaggerated amygdala reactivity and diminished OFC activation to faces expressing anger."

### **hypothalamus** — Still debated

Claim: The middle stage of the threat system that produces defensive attack in animals. The pathway was mapped in animals; that it does the same in human anger is assumed. Its onward target, the periaqueductal grey, is not drawn.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.55), ends above baseline (0.15).

- Blair RJR (2012). Considering anger from a cognitive neuroscience perspective. doi:10.1002/wcs.154 — review, full-text
  > "Animal work indicates that this progressive response to threat is mediated by a basic threat system that runs from medial amygdaloidal areas downward, largely via the stria terminalis to the medial hypothalamus, and from there to the dorsal half of the periaqueductal gray"
- Blair RJR (2012). Considering anger from a cognitive neuroscience perspective. doi:10.1002/wcs.154 — review, full-text
  > "This neural system, amygdala-hypothalamus-periaqueductal gray is thought to mediate reactive aggression in humans also"

### **insula** — Mixed evidence

Claim: Active on both sides when people feel angry: the main finding of a pooled analysis of studies of the experience of anger, read as an abstract only. The insula tracks the internal state of the body. In one study, its activity after a provocation also predicted how much people went on to dwell on it. Marked mixed because it rests on one pooled analysis in a field its authors describe as still debated.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.85), ends above baseline (0.5).
