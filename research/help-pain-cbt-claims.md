## What is reconstruction in this episode

All findings come from one small study of 13 people with different types of chronic pain, with no comparison group receiving another treatment; the authors say the changes may come from taking part in the programme rather than from the therapy itself. Pain intensity itself did not improve significantly, although other measures did. Most increases went above the level of people without pain, which the authors read as adaptation rather than a return to normal. Most changed areas were on one side only, as drawn. "Posterior parietal" is drawn on the inferior parietal cortex and "subgenual anterior cingulate/orbitofrontal" on the subgenual cingulate; both are approximations. These are averages across groups of people, not a picture of any one person's brain. Treatments help many people, not everyone, and in different ways. This is not medical advice: talk to a doctor or therapist about what is right for you.

## Notes from the research pass

- Before-after comparisons used grey matter density (unmodulated), not volume: "When no modulation was performed, results are referred to as changes in GMD".
- Pain did not reduce significantly ("pain and physical health were the only ones that did not show significant reductions"); stated in the caveat and last caption.
- The authors call the changes "likely adaptive, rather than a return to normal levels" and possibly "not specific to the cognitive therapy itself"; both in the caveat.
- Read in full: Seminowicz et al. 2013.

## The condition

### **dlpfc** — Mixed evidence

Claim: More grey matter after 11 weeks of CBT in one study of 13 people; the abstract describes it as on both sides, and the detailed list names the right side. Less catastrophising about pain went with more growth on the left but with less on the right. Brightness stands for the amount of grey matter (grey matter density), not activity.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.45), ends at baseline (0).

### **sma** — Mixed evidence

Claim: The only area with more grey matter than in people without pain before treatment, in a region-level test, and the only one with less after CBT, moving closer to people without pain. The paper places it on the right in one section and the left in another, so both sides are drawn. Brightness stands for the amount of grey matter (grey matter density), not activity.

On screen: peaks below baseline (-0.35), ends at baseline (0).

- Seminowicz DA, Shpaner M, Keaser ML, Krauthamer GM, Mantegna J, Dumas JA, Newhouse PA, et al. (2013). Cognitive-behavioral therapy increases prefrontal cortex gray matter in patients with chronic pain. doi:10.1016/j.jpain.2013.07.020 — study, full-text
  > "It is also worth noting that the one area that had reduced GM after CBT treatment – the SMA/pre-SMA – was the only region that had significantly greater GM in patients pre-CBT compared to controls (Fig 1), and after CBT the GM level decreased to a level more similar to controls, making this region the only one that showed normalization."
