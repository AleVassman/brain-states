## What is reconstruction in this episode

The findings come from two randomised trials, one of an 8-week course in healthy, non-clinical adults and one of a 3-day intensive course in stressed unemployed adults, and a review of 26 brain-imaging studies. The amygdala reacted less to positive pictures, but not to negative ones, after the 8-week course. The review reports increases in "activity, connectivity and volume" in several areas together, without separating them, so those are described, not drawn. These are not studies of an illness: it is linked to Acute stress, but the trials studied everyday or ongoing stress, not acute stress. The first part is drawn at rest. These are averages across groups of people, not a picture of any one person's brain. Treatments help many people, not everyone, and in different ways. This is not medical advice: talk to a doctor or therapist about what is right for you.

## Notes from the research pass

- Listed as a treatment for "Acute stress", although the trials studied stress in daily life, not acute stress in a scanner; the caveat says these are not studies of an illness.
- Gotink et al. 2016 report increases in "activity, connectivity and volume" of the prefrontal cortex, cingulate, insula and hippocampus without separating them; not drawn.
- Taren et al. 2015 also report that higher perceived stress goes with greater amygdala-sgACC connectivity across 130 adults (a relationship across people); described in the caption.
- Read in full: Taren et al. 2015, Kral et al. 2018. Read as abstract only: Gotink et al. 2016.
