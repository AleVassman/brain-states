## What is reconstruction in this episode

The blood-flow finding comes from one study of nine healthy volunteers whose blood sugar was lowered under careful medical control; another study of healthy volunteers found changes in more areas but, in the abstract read, without saying in which direction. Blood flow is used as a stand-in for activity. Overall blood flow to the brain fell slightly during low blood sugar while the areas drawn became relatively more active. These are mild, controlled episodes in a research setting.

## Notes from the research pass

- Hwang et al. 2018 (abstract) report that mild low blood sugar "altered activity in the caudate, insula, prefrontal cortex, and angular gyrus" in healthy people, without a direction in the abstract, so those areas are not drawn.
- People with type 1 diabetes who no longer notice low blood sugar "failed to show any hypoglycemia-induced changes in brain activity" (Hwang et al. 2018). Shown by returning the drawn regions to rest in that phase.
- Overall blood flow fell 6-8% (Teves et al. 2004); the regions drawn rose relative to the rest of the brain.
- Listed as "Low blood sugar" rather than "Diabetes": the brain findings are about a low-blood-sugar episode, not about diabetes in general.
- Read as abstracts only: all sources.

## Order of events

1. **Blood sugar falls** (minutes) — start of the episode.
2. **The body's alarm** (minutes) — order **reconstructed**: Blood flow was measured during steady low and normal blood sugar, not as it fell. Placing the alarm after the fall is how the procedure was done.
3. **When the alarm fades** (in some people with type 1 diabetes) — order **reconstructed**: This comes from a separate group of people with diabetes, compared at one time point.
4. **Blood sugar restored** (minutes) — order **reconstructed**: No source read measured the brain as blood sugar recovered. The return to rest is assumed.

## The condition

### **dacc** — Mixed evidence

Claim: Relatively more active during low blood sugar, on both sides, in one study of nine healthy volunteers. The study calls this area the medial prefrontal cortex and identifies it as Brodmann areas 24 and 32, parts of the anterior cingulate; the abstract does not say which part, so it is drawn on both the dorsal and the pregenual anterior cingulate. In people with type 1 diabetes who no longer notice low blood sugar, no response was found. Brightness stands for blood flow, not measured activity.

- Teves D, Videen TO, Cryer PE, Powers WJ (2004). Activation of human medial prefrontal cortex during autonomic responses to hypoglycemia. doi:10.1073/pnas.0307048101 — study, abstract-only
