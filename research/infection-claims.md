## What is reconstruction in this episode

These studies use a mild immune challenge in healthy volunteers and scan them two to three hours later. That is a stand-in for a real infection, and it does not produce a real fever. Fever itself is part of the same brain-directed response, but the sources read do not name the brain region that sets body temperature, so nothing is drawn for it. Most findings come from two small groups of volunteers, sixteen men in one and thirty-nine people in the other, and several were seen only while people did a particular task in the scanner. A pooled analysis confirms that inflammation goes with changes in these regions but, in the part read, does not say in which direction. How the brain recovers as the infection clears was not studied.

## Notes from the research pass

- Fever is not drawn. Saper et al. 2012 (abstract) say the sickness syndrome "includes fever, anorexia, sleepiness, hyperalgesia and elevated corticosteroid secretion" and that "Much of the sickness syndrome is mediated by prostaglandins acting on the brain", but the abstract names no brain region, and the volunteer studies used a challenge too mild to cause fever. The full text could not be downloaded.
- The first phase draws no region. Dantzer et al. 2008 (abstract): "innate immune cells produce pro-inflammatory cytokines that act on the brain to cause sickness behaviour", without saying where.
- The hypothalamus, amygdala, hippocampus and brainstem are listed by the pooled analysis (Kraynak et al. 2018) as consistently affected by inflammation, but the abstract gives no direction, so they are not drawn.
- No typical-response comparison: the volunteers were healthy, so this is the ordinary response. Each study compared the immune challenge with a placebo in the same or similar people.
- The two Harrison 2009 papers and Brydon 2008 each describe sixteen healthy men given typhoid vaccination and appear to report the same volunteers, so they are not three independent confirmations.
- Read in full: Harrison 2009 (mood). All others read as abstracts only.

## Order of events

1. **The alarm is raised** (the first hours) — start of the episode.
2. **Tired and slowed** (two to three hours in the studies) — order **reconstructed**: Volunteers were scanned once, two hours after the injection, and their mood and tiredness were asked about at two and three hours. The split into separate steps is a way of presenting those findings, not a measured sequence.
3. **Low mood, less pleasure** (around three hours in the studies) — order **reconstructed**: The mood drop was significant at three hours, an hour after the scan. Placing it after the tiredness is a way of presenting the findings; the studies did not time the two against each other.
4. **Getting better** (days (not studied)) — order **reconstructed**: No source read followed volunteers through recovery. The return to rest is assumed.

## The condition

### **insula** — Mixed evidence

Claim: Regions that represent the internal state of the body became more active under inflammation while volunteers did a demanding task, and their activity predicted how tired and foggy each person felt. The abstract of that study does not name the regions; a later paper by the same group names the insula. A pooled analysis lists the insula among regions consistently affected by inflammation.

  > "Performance of the Stroop task under inflammation activated brain regions encoding representations of internal bodily state."

### **substantia-nigra** — Mixed evidence

Claim: A small midbrain structure that supplies dopamine to the movement system. Its response was markedly disturbed after vaccination, and more activity there went with slower reactions and a stronger immune response. Drawn as more active on that basis; the first of those findings does not state a direction. A pooled analysis lists the midbrain among regions consistently affected by inflammation.

### **sgacc** — Mixed evidence

Claim: The more a volunteer's mood fell after vaccination, the more active this region was while they looked at emotional faces. The region is one implicated in depression, and the authors take this to suggest a shared basis for low mood in illness and in depression. From one study of sixteen men.

On screen: peaks above baseline (0.6), ends at baseline (0).

- Harrison NA, Brydon L, Walker C, Gray MA, Steptoe A, Critchley HD (2009). Inflammation causes mood changes through alterations in subgenual cingulate activity and mesolimbic connectivity. doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2009.03.015 — study, full-text
  > "Inflammation-associated mood deterioration correlated with enhanced activity within subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sACC) (a region implicated in the etiology of depression) during emotional face processing."

### **nucleus-accumbens** — Mixed evidence

Claim: The ventral striatum responded less to signs of a coming reward in people given a mild immune challenge than in people given a placebo, and that weaker response accounted for part of their drop in mood. From one study of thirty-nine people. A pooled analysis lists the striatum among regions consistently affected by inflammation.

On screen: peaks below baseline (-0.55), ends at baseline (0).

- Eisenberger NI, Berkman ET, Inagaki TK, Rameson LT, Mashal NM, Irwin MR (2010). Inflammation-induced anhedonia: endotoxin reduces ventral striatum responses to reward. doi:10.1016/j.biopsych.2010.06.010 — study, abstract-only
  > "subjects exposed to endotoxin, compared with placebo, showed greater increases in self-reported and observer-rated depressed mood over time, as well as significant reductions in VS activity to monetary reward cues"
- Kraynak TE, Marsland AL, Wager TD, Gianaros PJ (2018). Functional neuroanatomy of peripheral inflammatory physiology: A meta-analysis of human neuroimaging studies. doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.07.013 — meta-analysis, abstract-only
